Reflect on your BBO capstone project and answer the following questions in your post:

#### Initial codebase: how did you choose your starting codebase? Was it drawn from a public library, taken from previous work or built from scratch? Why did you select it? Please include a link to your codebase if it is publicly available so that your peers can explore your work directly.

I built the project codebase from scratch around the capstone data rather than starting from an existing black-box optimisation repository. In week 1 I only had two simple notebooks: one for browsing the initial data and one for choosing the first query points manually. From there I gradually added reusable Python modules for data loading, plotting, Bayesian optimisation, logistic regression, neural-network surrogates, and validation utilities. I used public libraries such as `scikit-learn`, `SciPy`, `NumPy`, and `PyTorch`, but the project structure and optimisation workflow were my own.

I chose this approach for three reasons. First, I wanted full visibility into every modelling decision rather than hiding the logic inside a large external BO framework. Second, the project evolved week by week, so a lightweight codebase that I could quickly adapt was more useful than a polished but rigid starter library. Third, building it myself made it easier to document why each function ended up with a different strategy.

#### Code modification: what changes did you make week by week? Why did you make those adjustments, and how did they affect your results? Which changes had the most significant impact?

The code evolved in clear stages.

In week 1 I used manual inspection for the 2D functions and maximin sampling for the higher-dimensional ones so that the first queries were as exploratory and diverse as possible. In weeks 2 and 3 I moved to a shared Bayesian optimisation setup using a Gaussian Process surrogate with UCB, mainly to make the process more systematic. That worked reasonably well for some functions, but it also showed the limitations of treating all eight functions the same way.

The first major change came in week 4, when I split out Function 1 and replaced the shared BO approach with logistic regression. BO kept drifting to extreme points because Function 1 was mostly flat with a narrow good region, so reframing it as a classification problem was a much better fit. In week 5 I upgraded that to non-linear logistic regression with polynomial features, which captured the boundary much better than the simple linear version.

The second major change came in weeks 5 and 6, when I moved the medium-dimensional functions toward exploitation and started separating workflows by function type. Functions 2, 4, 5, and 6 used exploitative BO after the early exploration phase, while Functions 7 and 8 switched to a PyTorch MLP surrogate. In week 6 I improved the neural-network pipeline by replacing random candidate sampling with gradient-based optimisation from multiple restarts, which made the high-dimensional search much more efficient.

From weeks 6 to 8 I added validation and better analysis rather than only changing the optimiser. Function 3 was isolated because its outputs were noisy, and I introduced progress-validation logic to flag when a method had stopped improving. In week 8 I added a structured results table showing output value, distance to the current best input, and whether each new result was improving. That changed the way I worked: instead of only trusting plots, I had a consistent way to spot which functions needed attention.

The final major refinement came in weeks 9 to 11. For Functions 2 and 5, and later the other noisy or unstable BO cases, I replaced the earlier exploitative GP setup with a more robust local trust-region BO pipeline: duplicate aggregation, a `WhiteKernel` to model noise, and local optimisation around the incumbent instead of unconstrained global maximisation. This reduced spurious GP peaks and stopped the optimiser drifting toward unrealistic suggestions. For Function 3 I also moved to a more explicitly noise-aware BO approach. For Function 1, I increased the target contour probability late on so the search stayed closer to the best-known region.

The biggest-impact changes were making the approach function-specific instead of forcing one BO method on every problem, switching Function 1 to non-linear logistic regression, using an MLP plus gradient optimisation for Functions 7 and 8, and adding validation tables before moving some BO pipelines to local trust-region, noise-aware optimisation. Those changes mattered because they improved both performance and decision quality: the models became better matched to the function type, and I had clearer evidence for when to keep exploiting and when to change strategy.

#### Final result: how did your score evolve in the final weeks? If you had more time or a fresh start, what would you do differently?

In the final recorded weeks in the repository, the overall picture was positive but uneven. The clearest late improvements came from the functions where the method matched the problem well and where I had introduced better validation. Between weeks 8 and 10, the best-so-far result improved for Function 3, Function 4, Function 5, Function 6, Function 7, and Function 8. Function 5 improved especially strongly late on, reaching a best recorded output of `8089.70` in week 10, while Function 4 improved to `0.4715` and Functions 7 and 8 reached `2.7826` and `9.9026` respectively. Function 2 peaked earlier in week 6, and Function 1’s best result came earlier as well, which is consistent with it being a harder boundary-finding problem that likely needed a better specialised strategy sooner.

So the final weeks were not a story of every function improving every round, but they were a story of the project becoming more reliable overall. Once I had the progress table, distance checks, and more robust local/noise-aware BO, I could focus effort on the functions that were stalling instead of treating all functions equally.

If I had more time, I would spend it on three things: deeper tuning of the neural-network surrogates for Functions 7 and 8; more systematic switching rules between UCB, EI, and local trust-region BO rather than changing methods manually; and better treatment of noisy or repeated observations earlier in the project. If I were starting again from scratch, I would specialise by function sooner instead of spending several weeks applying essentially the same exploratory BO pipeline to everything. I would also build the validation tables and progress checks earlier, because that structure ended up being one of the most useful parts of the whole workflow.

#### Trade-offs and decisions: what were the most significant trade-offs you faced? How did you balance exploration and exploitation or short-term improvements versus long-term strategies?

The central trade-off was exploration versus exploitation under a very small evaluation budget. In the first few weeks, I deliberately accepted weaker short-term scores in order to explore the search space and learn what kind of function I was dealing with. That was important because exploiting too early, especially with a misleading surrogate, could easily waste several weeks around the wrong region.

Later, the trade-off shifted. Once I had enough observations, the question was not whether to exploit, but how aggressively. For smoother functions I used exploitative BO because it made sense to refine around promising regions. But when exploitation started to drift, overfit repeated points, or chase artefacts in the GP surface, I had to trade raw aggressiveness for robustness by adding duplicate aggregation, observation-noise modelling, and local trust-region constraints. That sometimes meant moving more cautiously, but it made the search more defensible.

Another important trade-off was generality versus function-specific design. A single workflow for all functions was simpler to maintain, but it was not good enough. I eventually chose more complexity in the codebase in exchange for better performance: logistic regression for Function 1, noise-aware BO for Function 3, local trust-region BO for unstable GP cases, and neural-network surrogates for Functions 7 and 8.

There was also a practical trade-off between sophistication and interpretability. I could have pushed toward more complex models earlier, but I found that simple, understandable methods combined with careful validation often gave better decisions than a more complicated method I did not fully trust. In that sense, I tried to balance short-term gains with a longer-term strategy of building methods I could actually interpret, debug, and improve week by week.

##### Learning and application:
#### What was the most important lesson you learned from the BBO capstone project? How might you apply these lessons to future competitions or to real-world ML projects in your field?

The most important lesson was that understanding the data and the behaviour of the problem matters more than writing the most sophisticated code. My biggest improvements did not come from adding complexity for its own sake; they came from understanding when a method was mismatched to a function, checking whether results were actually improving, and then choosing a strategy that fit the evidence.

That is directly applicable to future competitions and real-world ML work. In practice, success often comes from good problem framing, careful validation, and disciplined iteration rather than from using the fanciest model available. In future projects I would spend more time early on building diagnostics, validating assumptions, and defining switching criteria for when a method is no longer working. I would also keep the habit of matching the method to the data-generating process rather than assuming one model should solve everything.

#### Looking back, what surprised you most about your process, your results or your fellow participants’ strategies?

What surprised me most was how much progress depended on organisation, clarity, and disciplined iteration rather than on inventing a very complicated algorithm. Clean notebooks, reusable scripts, validation tables, and clear week-by-week reasoning ended up being just as important as the optimisation methods themselves. Once the repository became more organised, it became much easier to spot what was working, what was stuck, and what needed to change.

I was also surprised by how effective relatively simple but well-matched methods could be. For example, a logistic-regression boundary model for Function 1 or a modest neural-network surrogate for the high-dimensional functions was often more useful than trying to force a single generic approach everywhere. More broadly, the project reinforced that good ML work is usually less about "coding harder" and more about understanding the problem, choosing appropriate tools, and being systematic in how you evaluate them.
